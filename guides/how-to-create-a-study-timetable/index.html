How to create a study timetable

A repeatable method you can set up in fifteen minutes and reuse every week.

A study timetable turns vague intentions into specific sessions. The aim is not a perfect-looking grid; it is a realistic plan you will actually follow. This guide walks through a simple method, then points you to a tool that builds the grid for you.

A step-by-step method

  1. List your subjects or topics. Write every subject you need to study, breaking large courses into topics if that helps.
  2. Find your real free time. Look at an ordinary week and note the hours you actually have after lectures, meals, rest and commitments — not an idealised schedule.
  3. Rank by priority and nearest exam. Mark which subjects are hardest, heaviest, or have the soonest assessment.
  4. Assign sessions. Place study sessions into your free slots, giving more to the higher-priority subjects while keeping every subject in the plan.
  5. Add breaks and revision. Leave short breaks between sessions and set aside time to revise what you studied earlier in the week.
  6. Review weekly. At the end of each week, adjust the plan based on what worked and what slipped.

Allocating time across subjects

Give more sessions to subjects that are harder for you, carry more credit units, or have an exam coming up soon. But balance matters: if you pour all your time into one subject, the others fall behind. A good rule is to ensure every subject appears at least once in the week before you add extra sessions to the difficult ones.

Breaks and revision

Studying in focused blocks with short breaks between them is easier to sustain than long, unbroken stretches. Spacing your revision — returning to a topic a few days later — generally helps retention more than cramming it all at once. Build a little review time into the plan rather than only studying new material.

Adapting to exam dates

Work backwards from each exam. As a subject's exam approaches, increase its share of your sessions and shift towards revision and practice questions rather than first-time learning. After an exam passes, free that time for the next priority.

An illustrative example

Illustrative only — adjust to your own timetable

Suppose you study Monday, Wednesday and Friday from 4:00pm to 6:00pm, in two 55-minute sessions with a 10-minute break. With Mathematics as a high priority and English and Physics as medium, a week might look like:

  • Monday: Mathematics (4:00–4:55), English (5:05–6:00)
  • Wednesday: Mathematics (4:00–4:55), Physics (5:05–6:00)
  • Friday: English (4:00–4:55), Mathematics (5:05–6:00)

This is just one arrangement to show the shape of a plan — it is not a guaranteed-ideal schedule. Your own subjects, free time and priorities should drive yours.

Common mistakes

  • Over-packing the day. A plan with no slack collapses the first busy afternoon.
  • Ignoring your energy. Schedule demanding subjects when you are actually alert.
  • No breaks. Back-to-back hours are hard to keep up.
  • Never reviewing the plan. A timetable you don't adjust quickly stops matching reality.
