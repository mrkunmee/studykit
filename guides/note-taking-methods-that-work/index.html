Note-taking methods that actually work

Stop copying slides word-for-word. Take notes built for revision.

Most students take notes by copying whatever is on the board or slide. It feels productive, but pages of transcribed text are hard to revise from. Good notes are selective, organised, and built to be tested against later. Here are methods that do that.

The Cornell method

Divide the page into three areas: a wide right column for notes during class, a narrow left column for cue questions you add afterwards, and a strip at the bottom for a short summary. To revise, cover the notes and answer the cue questions from memory. The layout builds active recall right into your notes.

Outlining

Capture structure with headings and indented sub-points. Outlining forces you to decide what's a main idea and what's a detail, which is itself a form of understanding. It works especially well for courses with clear hierarchy — definitions, categories, processes.

Make your notes active

Whatever the format, turn statements into questions. A line like "Photosynthesis occurs in the chloroplast" becomes "Where does photosynthesis occur?" A page of questions is a ready-made self-test; a page of statements is just something to re-read.

Review on a schedule

Notes you never revisit fade fast. Review new notes within a day, then space out further reviews over the following weeks — the spaced repetition principle. Build short review slots into your study timetable so it actually happens.

Common mistakes

  • Transcribing everything. If you're writing non-stop, you're not thinking.
  • Never rereading or testing your notes.
  • No structure — a wall of text is hard to revise.
  • Pretty over useful. Colour-coding is fine, but recall matters more than aesthetics.
