How to use past questions effectively

Make past questions a genuine practice tool — not a shortcut that fails you in the exam hall.

Past questions are one of the most useful study resources available, because they show the style, depth and recurring themes of a course's exams. Used badly, though, they become a trap — memorising answers without understanding leaves you stranded the moment a question is reworded. Here's how to use them well.

Why past questions help

They reveal what a course actually tests: which topics come up often, how questions are phrased, and how much depth is expected. That lets you focus your revision where it counts instead of studying everything equally.

Practise actively, not passively

Don't read a past question and its answer and move on. Attempt it first from memory, write your full answer, then check. The effort of retrieving the answer — even when you get it wrong — is what builds lasting memory. This is the same active recall principle that makes any study method work.

Look for patterns, not predictions

Across several years you'll notice topics that appear repeatedly and question types that recur. Use that to prioritise, but never assume a question will be "repeated" verbatim. Prepare to answer the topic in any form, not a specific question you've memorised.

Work under exam conditions

Once you know the material, do a set of past questions timed, without notes. This trains your pacing and exposes gaps you didn't know you had. Our Practice Quiz is a low-stakes way to test yourself, and reviewing every mistake turns each session into a revision list.

Avoid the memorisation trap

  • Don't rely on "expo" or leaked papers. It's dishonest, risky, and leaves you unable to answer an unseen question.
  • Don't memorise answer letters. Options get reordered; understanding doesn't.
  • Don't skip the ones you find hard — those are exactly where the marks you're missing live.
